# Common Abbreviations and Writing Conventions for Scientific Papers

## Overview

This guide summarizes where abbreviations are appropriate in scientific papers, the conventions that support clear writing, and commonly used examples. Requirements vary among journals and styles such as APA, AMA, IEEE, and Nature, so always follow the author guidelines of the target journal before submission.

## When to Use Abbreviations

- Terms that appear frequently, usually three or more times, and are familiar to readers can be abbreviated.
- Minimize abbreviations in titles and abstracts. If an abbreviation is necessary, define it at first use.
- In the main text, write the full term followed by the abbreviation at first use; use that same abbreviation consistently afterward.
- Tables and figure captions may use abbreviations directly when definitions are supplied in a note or abbreviation list.
- Avoid inventing abbreviations. When a shortened form is not well established in the field, prefer the full term.

For example, a remote-sensing study may calculate the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) and evaluate its time-series behavior. The term NDVI can then be used consistently for the remaining text.

## General Writing Rules

- **Consistency:** use only one abbreviated form for the same concept.
- **Capitalization:** follow the established term, for example DNA, GIS, and ANOVA; do not force a term to lowercase simply because it begins a sentence.
- **Plural forms:** do not add plural `s` to unit symbols (`5 km`, not `5 kms`), but plural nouns such as `PCs` are acceptable.
- **Punctuation:** `e.g.` and `i.e.` are often followed by a comma; outside parentheses, consider rewriting them as “for example” and “that is.”
- **Line breaks:** use a non-breaking space between a number and its unit when typesetting.

## Common General Abbreviations

| Abbreviation | Meaning | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| e.g. | for example | Write out “for example” in running prose when helpful; `e.g.` is common in parentheses. |
| i.e. | that is | Use it for clarification; do not confuse it with `e.g.`. |
| etc. | and so on | Use only when the list is already sufficiently clear. |
| vs. | versus | The full word “versus” is often more formal. |
| cf. | compare; see also | Introduces a comparative reference. |

## Statistical and Methodological Abbreviations

| Abbreviation | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| SD | standard deviation |
| SE | standard error |
| CI | confidence interval |
| OR / RR / HR | odds ratio / relative risk / hazard ratio |
| RMSE / MAE | root mean square error / mean absolute error |
| R² | coefficient of determination; `R^2` is also acceptable |
| AUC / ROC | area under the curve / receiver operating characteristic |
| ANOVA / ANCOVA | analysis of variance / analysis of covariance |
| GLM / GAM | generalized linear model / generalized additive model |

Writing notes:

- For P values and comparisons, use forms such as `P < 0.05`; distinguish `mean ± SD` from `mean (95% CI)`.
- Report effect sizes and intervals together with statistical significance whenever possible, for example `OR = 1.42, 95% CI 1.10–1.83`.

## Units and Symbols in the SI System

- Leave a space between a number and a unit: `25 km`, `5 mg/L`. Common exceptions include degrees, percentages, and temperature conventions: `45°`, `25%`, and `25 °C`.
- Unit symbols do not take plurals and do not change with capitalization: `1 m`, `10 m`.
- Use a decimal point rather than a comma as the decimal separator. For values of 1000 or greater, use a thin space or comma for grouping according to the journal style.
- Prefer a leading zero: `0.05`, not `.05`.
- Common units include `s`, `min`, `h`, `d`, `yr`; `m`, `km`; `g`, `kg`; `°C`; `Pa`, `kPa`; `W`, `kW`; and `Hz`, `kHz`.

## Abbreviations in Figures, Tables, and Section Headings

- **Figure and table captions:** if the journal allows it, use forms such as `Fig. 1` and `Table 2`; otherwise use the full form `Figure 1`.
- **Axis labels and units:** capitalize noun labels and follow SI symbols, for example `NDVI (dimensionless)` and `Precipitation (mm)`.
- **Section names:** use the conventional IMRaD structure—Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion—and avoid excessive abbreviations in headings.

## Abbreviations in References and Citations

- Common reference elements include `vol.` (volume), `no.` (issue), `pp.` (pages), `doi`, and `ed.` / `eds.` (editor or editors).
- In-text author references can use forms such as `Zhang et al., 2024`.
- Format bibliography entries consistently according to the required style, such as APA, IEEE, or Vancouver.

## Pre-Submission Checklist

- [ ] Is every first occurrence written as the full term followed by its abbreviation, with later use kept consistent?
- [ ] Are abbreviations in the title and abstract necessary and defined?
- [ ] Do figures, tables, and notes define their abbreviations or point to an abbreviation list?
- [ ] Are units, numerical values, and symbols consistent with SI and journal style?
- [ ] Are statistics, including P values, intervals, and effect sizes, formatted consistently?
- [ ] Are reference and in-text citation abbreviations formatted consistently?
